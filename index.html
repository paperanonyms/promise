Sim-to-Real-to-Field

Abstract

Persistent multi-robot patrolling is a complex real-world problem, requiring repeated observation of an environment while adapting to changes in layout and team composition. We present PRoMiSe, a multi-agent reinforcement learning policy for visibility-based persistent monitoring that commands body-frame velocities, with no patrol graph, task allocation or planner in the control loop. It combines a compact egocentric idleness summary and lidar ranges with attention over a variable number of teammates and recurrent temporal reasoning. A single policy transfers without fine-tuning to unseen simulated maps and teams of up to sixteen robots, and runs unmodified on Unitree Go2 quadrupeds at three physical sites. Matched indoor experiments with one to four robots show cumulative coverage within 0.6 percentage points of simulation. Outdoor deployments with two robots demonstrate coordinated patrolling in previously unseen environments, including an industrial site eight times the average training-map area. These results provide evidence of sim-to-real-to-field transfer for persistent multi-robot monitoring across layouts, scales, and team sizes.

Method

Approach

Observation

One shared policy, local inputs

Every robot runs the same weights on its own pose and heading, a sixteen-ray lidar scan, and an eight-sector histogram of how stale the space around it is, in its own body frame. No patrol graph, no task allocation, no assigned region.

Team encoder

Attention over a variable-size team

Teammates arrive as an unordered set of tokens carrying distance, bearing and relative heading. Cross-attention pools them into a fixed-width embedding, invariant to team size and ordering. That is what lets a policy trained with four robots run with sixteen.

Memory

Recurrence for what is already covered

A GRU carries what one observation cannot: where the robot came from and which regions it has already refreshed. Ablating it costs nothing at one robot and costs at every larger team, so the gain is coordination, not capacity.

Training

Centralized critic, decentralized execution

The critic sees the full team state during training only. Each robot then acts on what it senses and on the tokens its teammates broadcast, so a lost robot or a delayed message degrades the team gradually instead of breaking an assignment.

PRoMiSe in action

Every clip below plays at ten times real speed.

Training in simulation

Training runs on procedurally generated room layouts with one to four robots. Cells darken as they are observed and pale out as they go stale; the trail behind each robot is its recent path.

Generalization across team size

Training only ever sees one to four robots. The same checkpoint then runs with eight, twelve and sixteen without retraining, re-tuning or any reassignment: the team simply re-partitions the floor. Going from one to sixteen robots cuts average idleness from 304.3 s to 25.8 s and raises final coverage from 91.6% to 99.7%, so performance keeps improving beyond the training range.

Generalization across layouts

A single set of weights runs on layouts that share no structure with one another: open atria, rings, corridors, spokes, warehouse aisles, a parking lot, and real customer sites. All twelve are held out from training, all are patrolled by the same four-robot checkpoint.

On the field: the office, one team at a time

The same checkpoint drives physical Go2 quadrupeds in an office it has never seen. Nothing changes between these runs except how many robots are switched on.

Real-world deployments

The same checkpoint runs on physical Go2 quadrupeds at two outdoor sites, neither of them seen in training. The industrial test site is eight times the average training map.

Results

Key results

  • Scaling past the training range. From one to sixteen robots, average idleness falls from 304.3 s to 25.8 s and final coverage rises from 91.6% to 99.7%. Training only ever saw one to four.
  • Zero-shot transfer. One policy, trained entirely in a lightweight 2D simulator, runs on unseen maps and team sizes with no fine-tuning and no patrol graph.
  • Laboratory. Across one to four quadrupeds, cumulative coverage matches simulation to within 0.6 percentage points.
  • Field. At the parking lot, average idleness is 5.1% higher than simulation and coverage one point lower; at the industrial site, eight times the average training map, idleness differs by 0.3% and coverage is two points higher.
  • What the simulator needs. Obstacle geometry, sensor observations and area idleness are enough. Detailed legged dynamics and photorealistic rendering are not required for the behaviour to transfer.
